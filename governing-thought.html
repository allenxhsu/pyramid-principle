How to use this

Barbara Minto's observation is that readers take ideas in top-down — the answer first, then why it is true — while you arrive at the answer bottom-up, by cutting a problem apart and testing the pieces. Most documents read badly because they are written in the order the work happened. This tool keeps the two directions apart: steps 1–2 are the bottom-up work, steps 3–5 the top-down write-up, and one button carries what survived from the first into the second.

Learn it by doing. The walkthrough builds a small pyramid with you, one instruction at a time, on a real problem. About ten minutes.
The five steps
1 Define
State the gap. What the arrangement was, what disturbed it, the result you have (R1) and the result you want (R2). The question is not invented — it derives from R1 and R2, and everything downstream must bear on it.
2 Decompose
Cut the question into branches you can test. A why-tree when you don't know the cause; a how-tree when you do and need levers. Record the test, the source, and what it showed, then mark each branch supported or rejected.
3 Build
Write the governing thought, then the key line beneath it. Promote to pyramid does the first draft for you from the branches that survived.
4 Review
Twenty-six checks across problem definition, analysis structure, the introduction, and the pyramid's vertical and horizontal logic. Each explains the rule behind it.
5 Export
The pyramid is the source; memo, email, slide outline, talk track and problem brief are renderings of it, all in the same top-down order.
Minto's vocabulary
Governing thought
The single sentence at the apex. Everything below exists to support it.
Key line
The row directly beneath it — the reasons, which answer the reader's next question.
SCQA
Situation, Complication, Question, Answer. The introduction that turns your point into an answer.
R1 / R2
The undesired result you have and the desired result you want. The gap between them is the problem.
MECE
Mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive. No two points make the same claim; together they cover the ground.
Inductive (IND)
A group of like reasons — three facts of the same kind supporting one conclusion.
Deductive (DED)
Exactly three steps: a statement about the world, a statement about its subject, and the implication. Minto illustrates it with the classical syllogism — all men are mortal, Socrates is a man, therefore Socrates is mortal.
Ordering principle
Every group runs by time, by structure, or by degree. If you can't name which, it's a list rather than an argument.
Cutting principle
On the tree: what makes a set of branches exhaustive — by source, by stage, by who holds it.
Action title
A slide or section heading that is a full sentence asserting something, not a topic label.
The controls
Step dots
Hollow is untouched, half-filled is in progress, solid is sound.
Status pill
On a branch: click to cycle open → supported → rejected.
⌕
Opens a branch's test, source and finding.
IND / DED and “order?”
On any group in Build: its logic type and ordering principle.
STEP / ALL
In the margin: show findings for the step you're on, or the whole document.
× on a finding
Dismisses it. The checks are heuristics on English and will occasionally be wrong.
Data out

This is one module of an ontology toolkit, so the document leaves as a graph as well as as prose. Graph (JSON-LD) and Triples (CSV) in Export carry the same content: every idea as a typed entity, every relationship as a typed edge. The nesting the editor uses is an implementation detail; these state the semantics outright.

Entities
Document, Problem, Result (role undesired / desired), Question, Attempt, BranchGroup, Branch, Analysis, Conclusion, Claim (role governingThought / keyLine / support), Evidence, Check.
Edges
hasProblem, currentResult, targetResult, poses, attempted, hasAnalysis, hasBranch, testedBy, concluded, hasArgument, answers, supports, evidencedBy, derivedFrom, flaggedBy.
derivedFrom
The one that makes the rest analysable: a claim promoted from the tree keeps a link to the branch it came from, so an argument can be traced back to the analysis that produced it and the evidence that settled it.
Identifiers
urn:pyramid:<documentId>:<kind>:<nodeId>. Node ids are creation-ordered and stable across export, import and promotion, so documents can be merged without collision and revisions diffed.
Namespace
Terms resolve against @vocab, set by the NS constant near the graph code. Point it at your toolkit's namespace to align the vocabulary.
Checks
Lint findings are exported too — each Check carries its rule, severity and group, attached to the entity it is about. The readiness score rides on the Document.
Where this comes from

Barbara Minto developed this at McKinsey in the 1970s and set it out in The Pyramid Principle. The book runs in four parts — logic in writing, logic in thinking, logic in problem solving, and logic in presentation. Steps 3–5 here implement the first two; steps 1–2 implement the third. The walkthrough uses her own illustrations to explain each idea, paraphrased rather than reproduced, and gives you a separate small problem to practise on.

The 26 checks are an interpretation, not a checklist she published. They encode rules she argues for — one idea per box, three to five per group, MECE siblings, a non-disputable situation, an ordering principle for every group — as things a program can actually test. They are heuristics on English, so dismiss any that are wrong about your sentence.

Reverse-engineer a draft

Paste a memo, an email, or a bullet dump. The parser reads headings, bullet markers and indentation as structure, and falls back to one point per paragraph for flat prose. It also looks for an SCQA introduction hiding in the opening lines. Nothing is overwritten until you choose.

Logic audit

Sixteen deterministic checks, drawn from Minto's rules for the vertical relationship (does each idea summarise the ones below it?), the horizontal relationship (do ideas in a group belong to the same logical order?), and the introduction.

Deliverables

The pyramid is the source; these are renderings of it. Top-down order is preserved in every one — answer first, support second.
